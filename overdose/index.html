What to do if someone is overdosing

Fentanyl is now in much of the drug supply, including fake pills. Knowing the signs and having naloxone on hand saves lives.

Last reviewed November 3, 2026 · Sources listed at the bottom of this page

In an overdose, do this

  • Call 911 right away. Say the person isn’t breathing or won’t wake up, and give your location.
  • Give naloxone (Narcan): spray it into one nostril. If there’s no response in 2 to 3 minutes, give another dose.
  • Support their breathing with rescue breaths, or roll them onto their side.
  • Stay with them until help arrives. Naloxone can wear off before the drugs do.
  • You’re protected: Oregon’s Good Samaritan law means you can’t be arrested or prosecuted for drug possession for calling for help.

Step by step

  1. Check for signs. Won’t wake up, slow or no breathing, blue or gray lips or fingertips, gurgling or snoring sounds, tiny pupils.
  2. Call 911. Say the person isn’t breathing or won’t wake up, and give your location.
  3. Give naloxone (Narcan). Spray it into one nostril. If they don’t respond in 2 to 3 minutes, give another dose.
  4. Support their breathing. Give rescue breaths if you know how, or roll them onto their side.
  5. Stay with them. Naloxone can wear off before the drugs do, so wait for help to arrive.

Getting naloxone in Oregon

  • Buy it at most pharmacies without a prescription.
  • Ask your doctor, clinic or treatment counselor. Many can give it for free.
  • Syringe service and harm reduction programs give it out free.
  • Your county public health office can tell you where to find free naloxone nearby.
  • Your county’s Behavioral Health Resource Network offers harm reduction services, including naloxone.

Oregon’s Good Samaritan overdose law

Oregon law (ORS 475.898) is meant to make sure fear of arrest never stops anyone from calling 911. If you call for medical help during an overdose, you and the person overdosing can’t be arrested or prosecuted for:

  • Possessing drugs
  • Possessing drug paraphernalia
  • Being in a place where drugs are used

The law does not protect against other crimes, such as selling drugs, weapons charges or DUI, or against outstanding warrants, including federal warrants and warrants from other states.

This is general information, not legal advice.

Common questions

Will I get arrested if I call 911 for an overdose in Oregon?

Not for drug possession. Under Oregon’s Good Samaritan overdose law (ORS 475.898), if you call for medical help during an overdose, you and the person overdosing can’t be arrested or prosecuted for possessing drugs or paraphernalia, or for being in a place where drugs are used. The law doesn’t protect against charges like dealing, weapons, DUI, or outstanding warrants.

Do I need a prescription for naloxone in Oregon?

No. Naloxone nasal spray (Narcan) is sold over the counter at pharmacies and many stores without a prescription.

Where can I get free naloxone in Oregon?

Ask your doctor, clinic or treatment counselor, a local syringe service or harm reduction program, or your county public health office. Many give naloxone out for free. If you’re on insurance, a pharmacist can also bill it to your plan.

Can naloxone hurt someone who isn’t overdosing on opioids?

No. Naloxone only works on opioids and won’t harm someone who hasn’t taken them, so if you’re unsure, give it. It can cause sudden withdrawal symptoms in someone who uses opioids regularly.

How long does naloxone last?

Naloxone works for roughly 30 to 90 minutes, which can be shorter than the opioids in someone’s system. That’s why it’s important to call 911 and stay with the person even after they wake up.
